Gender, STEM, and Educational Stratification

  1. Mingming Li* and Wilfred Uunk. “Economic Opportunity and the Formation of Gendered STEM Aspirations: Security, Returns, and Accessibility.”

    Under external review American Journal of Sociology.

    Research summary

    This study distinguishes economic security from STEM-specific returns and accessibility. Drawing on TIMSS data across 61 countries and regions, it examines how these dimensions are associated with gender gaps in adolescents’ mathematics-related occupational aspirations.

    Draft available upon request

  2. Mingming Li* and Wilfred Uunk. “Gendered Entry, Convergent Exit: Socialization, Labor Market Signals and STEM Educational Trajectories.”

    Under external review European Sociological Review.

    Research summary

    Using NEPS linked to German Microcensus labor-market indicators, this study distinguishes initial STEM entry from later switching out of STEM. It examines how occupational interests, earnings premiums, and working hours relate to these two educational transitions.

    Draft available upon request

  3. Mingming Li*. “Macro-context associations with gender gaps in mathematics-related occupational aspirations vary by construct and geographic scale.”

    With editor Psychological Science.

    Research summary

    Using TIMSS, this study examines whether associations between macro-level conditions and gender gaps in mathematics-related occupational aspirations depend on how context is measured. It compares different constructs, geographic scales, and changes in girls’ and boys’ aspiration rates.

    Draft available upon request

  4. Mingming Li*. “Does Affluence Widen the Gender Gap in STEM Choices? Micro-Level Evidence from Germany.”

    Submitted after major revision Social Science Research.

    Research summary

    Using the German National Educational Panel Study (NEPS), this study examines how parental household income relates to women’s and men’s realized STEM major choices. It distinguishes income from wealth and considers academic performance, vocational interests, and family background.

    Draft available upon request

  5. Wilfred Uunk and Mingming Li*. 2026. “Does Societal Affluence Increase the Gender Gap in STEM Graduation? A Longitudinal Assessment.”

    Published Sex Roles 92(3), 19.

  6. Mingming Li* and Wilfred Uunk. “Matching, Mismatch, and Exit: Gendered Realization of STEM Credentials in Urban China.”

    Major revision British Journal of Sociology of Education.

    Research summary

    Using the 2018 China Household Income Project, this study examines gender differences in field-matched employment and paid work among urban STEM graduates. It shows how women’s concentration in medicine can conceal disadvantages within other STEM fields, while care responsibilities are associated with non-employment.

    Draft available upon request

  7. Mingming Li* and Wilfred Uunk. “Assets beyond Housing and Gendered Entry into Science and Engineering among Homeowning Families in China.”

    With editor Sociological Perspectives.

    Research summary

    Using the China Family Panel Studies, this study examines how financial and productive assets beyond housing relate to women’s and men’s entry into science and engineering. It distinguishes the composition of household assets from income and net wealth.

    Draft available upon request

Gender, Work, and Labor Markets

  1. Xinyi Wei, Ru Jia, and Mingming Li*. “Platform Access and Conditional Occupational Membership among Women Ride-Hailing Drivers in China.”

    With editor Work, Employment and Society.

    Research summary

    Drawing on initial and follow-up interviews with women ride-hailing drivers in China, this study examines how occupational recognition remains conditional after workers gain platform access. It traces how repeated evaluations by passengers and platforms interact with workers’ efforts to demonstrate competence and manage participation.

    Draft available upon request

  2. Tianhe Chen and Mingming Li*. “Algorithmic Collaboration and Negotiated Dependence: Gendered Claim-Making and Platform Governance in China’s Ride-Hailing Sector.”

    Under external review New Technology, Work and Employment.

    Research summary

    Drawing on 21 interviews with women ride-hailing drivers in China, this study examines how workers use dispatch systems, ratings, customer-service channels, and driver communities to negotiate workable conditions. It develops the concept of algorithmic collaboration to explain dependence on platforms alongside demands for safer and fairer governance.

    Draft available upon request

  3. Tianhe Chen and Mingming Li*. “Algorithmic Benevolent Sexism: Female Ride-Hailing Drivers and the Paradox of Protection in China.”

    Submitted after minor revision Sex Roles.

    Research summary

    Based on 21 interviews with women ride-hailing drivers in China, this study examines gendered evaluation, safety, and protective platform arrangements. It shows how drivers can experience protection as reassurance while also associating it with constraints on autonomy and earning opportunities.

    Draft available upon request

  4. Mingming Li and Xinxin Ma*. “Flexible but Penalized? Gender Wage Gaps in China’s Informal Sector.”

    With editor Social Science Research.

    Research summary

    Using the China Labor-force Dynamics Survey, this study examines gender wage gaps across the earnings distribution among informal workers while accounting for selection into informal employment. It finds the largest wage penalties near the bottom of the distribution and examines the roles of wage structures, family circumstances, and digital skills.

    Draft available upon request

  5. Mingming Li and Xinxin Ma*. “Employment Protection and Within-Couple Wage Inequality: Evidence from China.”

    With editor Journal of Family and Economic Issues.

    Research summary

    Using matched spouses from the China Labor-force Dynamics Survey, this study compares wages and employment allocation across couples with different combinations of employment protection. It connects the placement of protection within couples to wives’ earnings and husband–wife wage gaps.

    Draft available upon request

  6. Jiachen Han, Mingming Li*, Shi Li, and Yingying Hu. 2024. “The widening gender wage gap in the gig economy in China: the impact of digitalisation.”

    Published Humanities and Social Sciences Communications 11(1), 1639.

    Research summary

    Using the 2014, 2016, and 2018 China Labour-force Dynamics Survey, this study examines digitalization and gender wage gaps in gig work while accounting for selection into employment. Digital development is associated with smaller wage gains for women than for men, with larger gaps among older and married workers.
